# Kirkenes

## The Five-Kilometer Philosophy

The sign says "5km." That's it. Just a piece of cardboard, black marker, nothing fancy. The logic is simple: everyone on this Arctic highway is going at least five kilometers. Which means everyone can stop. Which means I'm not asking for much. Which means the barrier disappears.

I've been working here as a tour guide—dog sledding, northern lights, the whole package. Good money, warm beds, predictable days. But the season is winding down, and something in me needs to move.

A car pulls over within ten minutes.

"Five kilometers?" The driver looks confused. "Where are you actually going?"

"I actually don't have anywhere to go," I tell him. "As you're driving, we'll see if there's anything interesting."

He grins. This is the right answer. He's going to Vardo, his hometown—further north, further east, the edge of everything. Would I like to come?

I would.

# Vardo

## The Birdwatcher's House

We talk for hours. When we arrive, he asks where I want to be dropped. I say anywhere is fine, I'll find somewhere to camp. He looks at me like I've suggested sleeping in the sea.

"I have something better."

The something better is a three-story house belonging to a Swiss friend who visits once or twice a year for birdwatching. No electricity, but walls and a roof and zero-degree warmth instead of whatever frozen nightmare awaits outside. The third floor has a telescope pointed at the cliffs where seabirds nest.

I stay three nights. Evenings pass by headlamp, reading my Kindle, eating discount supermarket hauls—oranges, tuna, vanilla yogurt, chocolate. Norway has price wars this time of year. Even broke backpackers can eat well if they know where to look.

One afternoon I bump into tourists I'd guided on a dog sledding tour days earlier, back when I was working in Kirkenes. They're on a coastal cruise now, seeing the Arctic from the comfortable side. We laugh at the improbability. The world up here is very small.

# Honningsvag

## The Theater Bus and the Snowstorm

Vardo to Nordkapp. I start hitchhiking at five in the morning.

First ride: a bakery van. The driver has stories for every kilometer and baked goods he keeps pressing into my hands. Pastries, bread, more pastries. Eventually I have to refuse—my backpack is gaining weight I can't afford to carry.

Second ride: a Mercedes two-seater. Expensive cars almost never stop. When it pulls over, I assume they want directions. They don't. They just want company.

Third ride finds me at a gas station where a bus driver waves me over. I shake my head, point to my pockets. "No money, no money." He waves more insistently. I climb in.

The doors open to chaos. Loud music. Laughter. Conversations overlapping in languages I half-recognize. Inside: a Sami theater group on tour with breakdancers from Guatemala. This is not a sentence I expected to write.

"Where are you going?" someone asks. "There's nothing in five kilometers."

"I don't care. I'm going with you wherever you're going."

"We're going to Nordkapp. For a play."

Perfect.

They give me a pamphlet about the production. I spend the next many minutes memorizing names and faces, matching them to the people around me. When I later address someone by name they haven't told me, their surprise turns to delight. Small effort, big return. This is the hitchhiker's currency.

The road to Nordkapp is dangerous. We wait for a convoy of snowplows. Trucks have flipped on the highway—the wind doesn't ask permission up here. When we finally arrive, everyone disperses to hotels. I go looking for a camping spot.

It's minus twenty. The wind is biblical.

I stop a stranger. "Excuse me—any churches nearby? Or overpasses? Somewhere with shelter?"

He shrugs. "Good luck."

I start walking. Then he calls out: "I think that person wants to talk to you."

I turn. The bus driver is running toward me.

"My hotel room has two beds," he says, catching his breath. "You're welcome to sleep there."

I gladly accept, meanwhile wondering if the bed was actually there to begin with. I never ask if he upgraded the room. I never ask if he paid extra. I don't want to rob him of the pleasure of an anonymous good deed. Some kindnesses are better left unexamined.

That night, lying in a warm bed I didn't earn, I feel two things simultaneously: relief, and the strange disappointment of a character-building experience narrowly avoided. Type-two fun, denied.

The next morning, instead of touring the town, I find the lighting and sound crew and offer to help set up the stage.

They stare at me.

"In two months of touring," one of them says, "no performer has ever offered to help."

I'm not a performer, I tell them. I'm just some guy from the road.

They put me to work. Cables, speakers, the geometry of light. Afterward, they take me to a pub for beer and pizza. We talk about nothing important. It's exactly right.

The play that evening is beautiful—part Sami language, part breakdance, part something I don't have words for. Art that doesn't need translation.

# Kautokeino

## Making Friends

Without a plan, I plan to stop in Alta. Seems a reasonable stop along the way back south. The stage crew guy has other ideas.

"This is the best time of year for Kautokeino," he says. "There is our annual Sami festival. You should come. Stay at my house."

His mother and brother live there. The brother takes one look at me.

"Why did you bring some homeless guy from the street into the house?"

Fair question. I offer to leave, continue hitchhiking. My friend insists I stay. "He'll warm up."

He does. After a day or two of shared meals, something shifts. By the end, the brother and I are closer than my original host and I ever were. Skepticism transformed into something like family.

The festival is seven days away. To pass time, I help at his mother's cafe—a one-woman operation where the dishes pile up all day. I spend hours at the sink, meditating to music, scrubbing plates. By closing time, everything is clean.

She's beyond thankful. "What a wonderful person you brought home," she tells her son. I don't think I'm wonderful. I just know how to wash dishes.

Festival tickets cost a hundred euros. My budget is ten euros a day. The math doesn't work.

I find the organizers. "I really want to go. I have no money. What can we do? Any help I can give?"

"Come tomorrow at eight. We'll work something out."

I spend ten hours setting up the concert stage. Free lunch. New friends. Lessons in lighting I didn't know I wanted.

In exchange: a festival pass.

My friend lends me a Sami costume to wear. The moment I step outside, everyone notices I don't belong. Which means everyone wants to talk. Where are you from? How did you end up here? Every second person hands me a beer. I accumulate so many I start redistributing them to strangers.

"Here. Beer for you."

The hitchhiker's economy in reverse.

The night before I leave, the brother—the one who wanted me gone—is having relationship troubles. He wants to break up with his wife. He's drunk and certain.

I talk him down. "Go to sleep. Let things settle. Decide tomorrow when you're clear."

The next morning, he thanks me. He wanted to stay with her. He just needed someone to say wait, I guess.

When I finally leave, both brothers try to give me everything they think I need. Food. T-shirts. Socks. I wonder what that says. I refuse most of it, not all. We hug. Goodbye.

The 5km sign is still in my bag, slightly more battered now. It doesn't promise a destination. It promises a direction. That's enough.

What I'm learning: vulnerability opens doors. Not just passing through places but being _with_ people—memorizing their names, washing their dishes, setting up their stages. Showing up. Offering something. Receiving what's given.

Strangers becoming friends through the simple act of saying yes.
