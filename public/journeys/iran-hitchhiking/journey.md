# Tehran

## Three Cars in a Row

I meet this New Zealand cyclist at the Tajikistan airport. He's packing down a touring bike, probably tired off the Pamirs. We hit it off waiting for our flight. On the plane, an Iranian man sits nearby—also returning from Tajikistan. He offers to help negotiate the taxi at the other end. "It can be tricky," he says.

Immigration is deserted. The line is closed, lights off, the hall empty except for us two. Staff seem confused by our presence. "What do you want?" they ask. They open immigration just for us.

The money exchange surprises me. Online rates suggested I'd get less—but I walk away with double what I expected. Four prepaid debit cards too, since Iran isn't connected to the international banking system, we need to convert beforehand all the money we need.

The taxi drama begins immediately. Our Iranian friend negotiates: 40,000 for the trip to Tehran. Memory fails me, but let's say that is the amount. The driver spots my debit cards—worth 400,000 each—and starts gesturing for me to pay with a card instead. We make clear we know the value. He tries a few more times.

Near the hostel, maybe a kilometer or two away, the driver stops. Claims the roads are closed. They're not—just jammed with traffic. Now he demands 40,000 each instead of total. We argue. Passers-by notice. They ask for our version of events.

What happens next still amazes me. The locals start shouting at the driver. It nearly gets physical. We pay the agreed amount, walk two kilometers to the hostel with our bags. The "hostel" turns out to be a hotel with only private rooms. Small, but okay.

The next morning, I'm walking to the metro. Not far from the hotel. A car stops beside me.

"Hello my friend, do you need help?"

I tell him I'm just going to the metro. "Ah, it's right ahead, couple blocks."

Seconds later, another car stops. Same question.

Then a third car. I think it must be a prank. But no—this is just Iran. Every driver sees a foreigner and genuinely wants to help.

At the bazaar, I order a snack. The price shows "1/500." I think: one item for 500. I pay 2,000, expecting 1,500 back. I get 500. I argue with the seller, certain I'm being scammed.

He insists it's correct.

Then it clicks. In Iran, the slash separates thousands. The dot means zero. The price was 1,500, not "one for 500." I've been arguing over my own confusion.

![Tehran cityscape](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-tehran.webp)

Near the Museum of Holy Defense, I order ice cream. The server asks where I'm from. Brazil, I tell him. I mention the trip briefly. He hands me the ice cream and refuses payment.

"This is a gift from Iran to you, my friend."

A commercial establishment refusing money. It still marks me to this day.

![Down with USA written somewhere in Tehran](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-down-with-usa.webp)

That evening, I meet an acquaintance I'd crossed paths with in Moscow. We go to a place with a pub vibe—dark corners, soft music, the atmosphere of a bar. But they only serve tea and coffee. It's surreal. Clearly trying to recreate the bar experience without the alcohol.

While talking about my plans, my friend warns me: Iranians don't know the concept of hitchhiking. He writes a letter in Farsi explaining what I'm doing, asking for rides.

![With my Tehran friend](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-tehran-cser.webp)

The next day, I take a bus to the highway and start thumbing with the letter. The first car stops—confused, but helpful after reading it. Iranian roads are amazing, smooth. I barely notice the driver doing 160, 180 kilometers per hour. He's not going to Isfahan, so he drops me on the highway.

Second ride: the guy says "very easy, just take the bus." Drops me at a bus station despite my protests. I have to walk four kilometers back to the highway with my heavy backpack.

I stop at a mechanical shop for cardboard. Ask them to write "Isfahan" in Farsi. They do. They also give me an apple.

Back on the highway, thumbing with my sign. A car stops. The driver speaks English.

"I'm not going to Isfahan. Just wanted to say your sign is upside down."

Ops. I write "THIS SIDE UP" with an arrow on the back.

Within thirty minutes, I get a ride to Isfahan.

# Isfahan

## The Backup Host

I arrive in Isfahan with a Couchsurfing host arranged. We meet up, but he says maybe he can't host me for sleeping after all.

"But don't worry, come. Let's go eat kebab."

So we eat kebab. Then we go to a friend's place for shisha. Several people are there, all curious about my stories. Where have I been? Where am I going? The questions flow with the smoke.

My original host leaves early. But the owner of the place says I can sleep there.

![With my Isfahan host](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-isfahan-host.webp)

The next day, my original host takes me on a tour. He used to work in the gold market—his brother still has a shop there. We wander through the old bazaar, past gold shops glittering in the dim light.

![In the Isfahan bazaar](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-isfahan-gold-market-cser.webp)

We visit the Jame Abbasi Mosque. The architecture staggers me.

![Isfahan architecture](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-isfahan.webp)

Afterward, I wander back into the bazaar and find myself in the carpet section. I'm just looking around when a seller waves me inside.

"Come, have tea."

Turkey flashbacks. I know this script. You go in, they show you carpets, they pour tea, and then you spend an hour trying to escape without buying something.

I mention I'm backpacking, that I have no money, that I'm not buying anything.

"No problem, no problem. Just look. Have tea."

So I go in. They show me carpets—some of the most beautiful I've ever seen. Silk threads catching the light, pomegranate skin coloring, patterns that took months to weave. They explain how they're made, the different knots, the regions, the families who still weave by hand. They ask about my trip, my stories. We drink tea.

Then I thank them and stand up to leave.

And they let me go.

No arguing. No "just one small one." No guilt. No following me to the door. Just a smile. This is not Turkey.

I spend the rest of the day at the park nearby, watching families picnic, trying to absorb it all.

"Isfahan is half the world," the Persian saying goes. Standing here, I believe it.

# Ghoortan Citadel

## A Thousand-Year-Old Welcome

I'm hitchhiking toward Yazd when I get a ride with a very nice man. What follows becomes the peak of hospitality in my entire Iran journey.

He insists we stop at Ghoortan Citadel, where he lives. The place is a thousand years old, ten kilometers outside Varzaneh, next to the Zayanderud river. Old houses, mosques, fourteen watch towers, hammams, animal coats. All mud-brick. Historically rich and still alive—some families remain in the southern corner, like my host.

We walk through abandoned houses together. The walls are crumbling but the scale is still there. Centuries of life, now mostly wind and dust.

![Iranian hospitality](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-old-city.webp)

Back at his house, his mother has made breakfast. The whole family is present. I sit on the floor with him. His mother, wife, and two daughters stand across the room.

Of course there's a watermelon on the floor.

Tea. Yogurt. More food than I can eat. We talk about culture, about Iran. Like everyone here, they want to know what I think of their country.

![Iranian hospitality](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-hospitality-2.webp)

It turns out he's both an English teacher and a PE teacher at the local school. He asks how I stay in shape while traveling. Before I can answer, he makes us do push-ups and squats together. In the living room.

His daughters whisper to each other. He asks if I have a wife. A girlfriend, I tell him. He asks for a photo of my "gorgeous girlfriend."

The 20-something year old daughters want to see too.

I show them the photo.

He translates their verdict: "You can do better."

![More hospitality](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-hospitality.webp)

I tell him I should leave for Yazd. He shakes his head.

"You cannot go now. I called my brother. He's coming from Tehran to meet you with my nieces."

His brother drives hours just to meet this random traveler passing through. More conversation, more food, lunch with the family. I finally leave around two or three in the afternoon.

He takes me to the road. Puts my backpack down. The first car appears on the horizon.

He walks to the middle of the road.

He waves both hands until the car stops. Has a conversation with the driver. Turns to me.

"He's taking you."

The driver didn't have much choice.

![And more](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-hospitality-3.webp)

# Salt Lake

## You Can't Make This Up

It's too late to reach Yazd, so I stop in Varzaneh and stay at a hostel. The next morning brings a minimal breakfast. I ask the manager what there is to do around town.

He tells me a tour is leaving soon—salt flats, desert, Milky Way viewing at night.

I tell him it's out of my budget. I'll try hitchhiking instead.

He's skeptical. "Very hard. These places are off the path. Nobody around. Very touristic."

I decide to try anyway.

The first car that stops: locals visiting from Tehran. A young couple.

They want to see the salt flats and the desert.

You can't make this up.

I join them. We drive out to the salt lake together. The white stretches to the horizon, blindingly bright under the midday sun.

We arrive at the salt flats and there's the hostel manager—who is also the tour guide. He's amazed to see me.

He offers to guide all three of us for free. My driver is delighted to have a free tour guide. I'm delighted to have found exactly the ride I needed.

The slimy water from the flats feels like oil to the skin, hardly goes away until it dries.

# Varzaneh Desert

## The Rave and the Truck

At the end of the tour, we reach the Milky Way viewing spot. Everyone is heading back to town.

I ask them to leave me behind.

My plan: camp somewhere in the desert. I have food and water. Getting out is a problem for tomorrow.

I walk ten or fifteen minutes into the dunes, away from the road. Find a nice spot, beautiful and remote. Set up my tent. Ready to sleep under the stars.

![The desert](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-desert.webp)

![My camping spot](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-desert-camping.webp)

I doze off. Wake up around eleven at night.

Three or four full buses have arrived at the spot, ten or fifteen minutes away. About the same minutes I walked from. A rave party in the desert. Music thumping across the sand. And then: 4x4 trucks start going crazy in the dunes.

One truck is climbing the dune I'm on. Headlights coming straight at my tent.

I try to scream. Nothing comes out.

I'm frozen. Mouth open. Just watching.

The truck drives straight at me.

A few meters away: sharp turn. It misses me.

I still don't know if it was an intentional scare or a genuine near-miss. If that truck didn't turn in time, that would surely have been the end of me.

I make it through the night.

The next morning, I pack up and head to the highway. Get a ride with a truck delivering supplies nearby. Getting out of Varzaneh isn't easy—few rides, longish waits. Eventually I find a driver going to Kerman.

Executive decision: skip Yazd, go straight to Kerman.

# Kerman

## The Professional Pomegranate Eater

The driver stops on the way to buy chicken and a pomegranate. Standard roadside purchases, apparently. What isn't standard is what he does next.

He splits the pomegranate in half. Then he bites directly into the cut side, like eating an apple, although from the inside. No picking out seeds. Just teeth into fruit, one hand still on the wheel. He does this at highway speed with the confidence of someone who's been eating pomegranates this way his entire life.

He hands me the other half.

I look at it. I look at my shirt. I think about attempting this technique. I decide I'd rather not arrive in Kerman looking like a crime scene. So I eat like an amateur. Shame.

By the time we reach the city, I'm feeling off. Not dramatically sick, just... wrong. That vague heaviness behind the eyes. The throat that isn't quite a sore throat yet. I find a cheap single room hotel and tell myself I'll feel better in the morning.

I don't feel better in the morning. The stuffy nose has become concrete. The vague heaviness is now a splitting headache. This is not hitchhiking weather.

I hire a private car for a day trip instead. Surprisingly affordable. Sometimes you just have to accept that standing on a highway with your thumb out isn't going to happen today.

# Coloured Mountains

## Where the Earth Shows Its Palette

The day trip takes me to the Coloured Mountains first. The name sounds like marketing, the kind of thing you'd see on a tourist brochure. It isn't.

The mountains are genuinely colorful. Bands of red and orange and yellow stripe across the slopes like geological layer cake. Iron, copper, sulfur—the earth's makeup bag spilled across millennia of erosion. I walk around feeling like I'm inside a painting, which would be more enjoyable if my head weren't pounding.

We visit some gardens next, palace grounds from another era. Beautiful. I take photos. I can barely focus on them.

# Kalout Desert

## The Hottest Place on Earth

Then we head to the Kalout Desert, in the Dasht-e Lut. "Hottest place on earth," the driver says. Satellite measurements have recorded ground temperatures here above 70 degrees Celsius. Right now, in late October, the weather in town is mild. But out here in the desert, it's still properly hot. I can only imagine what summer feels like.

We pass underground constructions—remnants of people who actually lived in this heat. Built down instead of up, seeking coolness beneath the sand. Clever. Desperate. Both.

On the way back, we spot a figure by the road. A hitchhiker, trying to get out of the desert. It's getting late, and out here "late" means "no more cars until tomorrow, maybe."

I ask the driver if we can pick him up. He wants money. They argue. Eventually they make a deal—the hitchhiker rides for free, I am not sure.

I ask the driver to drop me at the bus station. The sickness has won. I'm not hitchhiking anywhere tomorrow. I book an overnight bus to Shiraz instead.

# Shiraz

## Too Much Hospitality

On the bus, I message my Couchsurfing host in Shiraz. I explain that I can't stay with him—I'm too sick, I need a hotel, I need to sleep for approximately one hundred hours. I won't be a good guest.

He replies: "Absolutely not. I will pick you up at 6am."

This is not a suggestion.

He's there at the bus station when I stumble off. I tell him I just need a pharmacy, medicine, and rest. He nods. We go to his place and greet the family, then go to a pharmacy. He buys the medicine. I try to pay. He refuses.

Then he takes me to "the best bakery around." There's a line turning the corner. We wait. I take paracetamol and eat breakfast. I tell him I need to sleep.

![With my Shiraz host](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-shiraz-cser.webp)

He considers this. "Let's go to my garden. Good place to relax."

The garden is his country house, outside the city. We put my backpack in the car and drive. On the way, we stop at his workplace. A colleague sees me and declares that the best thing for a foreigner is Persepolis.

I try to argue. I mention how I feel like death. I mention the fever. I mention that standing upright is currently a challenge.

I lose the battle.

# Persepolis

## Persepolis, who am I?

I was approximately 40 percent alive, my host insisted we visit.

The entrance fee is around $20 USD. Not insignificant.

My host says he'll get me in without paying. He makes me memorize three or four words in Farsi, which I am to speak if asked anything. I repeat them until he's satisfied. We approach the entrance.

He talks to the staff. They look at me. They chat. They nod. We walk through. No payment.

I'm impressed. I ask how it worked.

My host grins. He'd told them I was his "retarded brother."

The phrase I memorized? "I don't understand. I'm retarded."

I walk around Persepolis, one of the great archaeological sites of the ancient world, the ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Empire where Darius and Xerxes once held court—and half the time I'm thinking about the fact that I entered as mentally disabled, and the other half I'm thinking about how miserable I feel.

The ruins are stunning. Columns reaching toward the sky. Stone reliefs depicting delegations from across the empire. Lions and bulls and warriors carved with precision that seems impossible for 2,500 years ago.

I appreciate about 50 percent of it. The other 50 percent is dedicated to not vomiting on UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

# Shiraz

## Finally, the Garden

After Persepolis, my host stops for supplies. Each item from a specific shop. Pineapple from one place. Bananas from another. Butter from a third. Bread from a fourth. Every ten minutes I mention how shit I feel. He nods sympathetically and drives to the next shop.

Finally we reach the garden. He prepares food. I don't feel like eating. I take a pillow from a kitchen chair, crawl under the kitchen table, and try to sleep where nobody can see me.

More people arrive. Loud electronic music starts.

Every beat is a hammer to my skull. I lie under the table, hidden from view, while a party erupts around me. Eventually I give up and sit with the people. My host is already drunk from homemade wine and some sort of rakja.

Around seven or eight in the evening, the guests start leaving. My host decides to drive home. I thank God. Finally. Rest.

On the way home, he stops somewhere. "This is my friend from Brazil. He has nice stories." I have to tell stories. I tell stories while my fever climbs.

Half an hour later: "Now let's go home."

We get in the car. He stops at his old workplace. Same thing. "This is my friend from Brazil. Tell them about your travels." I tell them about my travels. I can barely see straight.

We get back in the car. He stops again. "Just need to say hi to a friend then we go home."

I ask, very calmly: "Can I get something from my backpack in the trunk?"

He pops the trunk. I take out my backpack. I put it on. I look at him.

"I cannot do this anymore. See you later."

I cross the street. I wave at a taxi.

My host speaks to the taxi driver in Farsi. He's asking him not to take me. I can see it in his face, in his gestures.

The taxi driver looks at me. I say: "Go-go-go. Hotel. Go-go."

He believes my desperation more than whatever my host is saying.

As the taxi pulls away, I hear my host shouting in the street behind us: "My friend, come back! I love you, come back my friend!"

## Three Days of Hell

I go straight to a hotel. I check in. I don't leave for three days.

The routine is simple. Go downstairs. Get water. Go to bathroom. Vomit. Other bathroom activities. Sleep. Repeat.

My fever hits 39.5 degrees. I moan. I sleep. I wake up feeling worse. I sleep again.

My host tries many times to find which hotel I'm at. I don't tell him.

## The Pink Mosque

After three days, I wake up and feel... okay. Not good. But okay. Functional. Human-adjacent.

I go downstairs and order a kebab. It stays down. This feels like victory. I look around and the convenience store has beer! What? Erm...you got me for a second. It is apple juice in beer cans.

![No beer, but endless tea](/journeys/iran-hitchhiking/img-no-beer.webp)

The next day I visit the Pink Mosque, the Nasir ol-Molk. I've seen photos. The stained glass windows throwing colored light across the carpets, turning the interior into a kaleidoscope every morning.

The photos don't prepare you. I'm still not 100 percent—maybe 70, if I'm being generous, but standing in that light, watching the colors shift and blend, I forget about the fever. Just for a moment.

# Goodbye Iran

## The End Comes Early

Unfortunately, this is where the hitchhiking ends—a few days earlier than planned. My body has made an executive decision, and that decision is "no more standing on highways."

I make plans to continue to Armenia. Train from Shiraz to Tehran, then switch to a bus bound for Yerevan.

The Shiraz host showed me hospitality that was genuinely extraordinary. The problem was that I couldn't enjoy any of it. Every gift felt like a challenge. Every kindness was another hour I wasn't sleeping.

If I hadn't been sick, it would have been one of the highlights of the trip.

Actually, it still was. Just in a different way than intended. The kind of story that's terrible to live and wonderful to tell.

Iran surprised me at every turn. The drivers stopping to help. The free ice cream at the museum. The family breakfast at the citadel. The carpet sellers who let me leave. The relentless, overwhelming, occasionally suffocating generosity of strangers.

Goodbye, Iran. I came with hopes and left with stories. And a lingering cough. And a phrase in Farsi I will never use again.
